## Core finding and strongest competing readings

This is substantive favorable evidence of a heat-transfer model compared with measured steel temperatures from two identified full-scale furnace tests. It is not merely an assumed-temperature structural demonstration. Several lower-chord and mid-web comparisons are reasonably close under the paper's stated metrics, and its assumptions and difficulties are discussed openly. Those observations defeat a sweeping claim that no physical temperature comparison exists.

The narrower ceiling is important: measured unexposed slab temperatures enter as boundary conditions; input assumptions and several data-selection rules are not independently validated here; the later SFRM-loss scenarios are explicitly trial-and-error fits; no held-out protocol or complete uncertainty analysis is supplied; and the paper does not perform a structural sag comparison. The existence of this later conditional thermal comparison therefore does not, by itself, identify or reproduce the FE calculation behind a 2005 sag-consistency statement or validate a WTC 7 causal chain. Conversely, being conditional, partly calibrated, or later does not nullify its genuine comparison of computed steel temperatures with observations.

## Page-pinned source observations

### Identity, specimens, and observed temperatures — pp. 1–12

- Pages 1–4 place the work in a sequential fire/heat/structure framework, but this paper's executed analysis concerns heat transfer under prescribed standard-fire exposure. It explicitly leaves uncertainty estimation for member temperatures as future work (p. 4). The abstract and conclusion describe thermal results as inputs that can subsequently be used for structural analysis, not a structural analysis already reported here.
- Pages 4–6 describe two 35 ft (10.7 m) full-scale assemblies tested at UL Canada: Test 1 restrained, Test 2 unrestrained. These are not all four historical furnace tests. Nominal SFRM is 0.75 in (19 mm), with reported application tolerance about 1/16 in; the floor has a 1.5 in profiled deck and concrete 4 in above the deck crest. Page 6 describes welded/support-frame restraint versus slotted bolts and a slab/frame gap. There is no mechanical restraint degree of freedom in the thermal model comparison merely because the two physical specimens have different restraint labels.
- The nominally similar specimens are not an exact controlled pair differing solely in restraint. Table 2 (p. 6) reports different concrete density, air content, water addition, slump, and strength, with 193 versus 189 days from casting to testing. For example, the printed 28-day strengths are 4,177 versus 2,937 psi. These are source-reported specimen differences, not a quantified causal attribution of their temperature differences.
- Pages 6–8 identify 24 furnace thermocouples, additional plate/aspirated measurements, and eight steel thermocouples per instrumented section: two upper-chord, two mid-web, and four lower-chord sensors, 88 steel sensors overall. Figures 3–4 identify the sensor positions and north/south location labels. The presence of measured gas histories does not mean they were used as the model's fire input: p. 15 specifies the ASTM E119 curve.
- Pages 8–10 associate loud reports, abrupt temperature increases, and post-test observations with possible concrete spalling/SFRM dislodgement, especially around Test 1 locations C/E/I at about 50–60 min and Test 2 C around 70 min. Those observations support the loss hypothesis but do not directly measure the later fitted removal lengths, thicknesses, or exact event times.
- Table 3 (p. 9) records substantial sensor variation, notably upper-chord Test 1 at 120 min, 358.1 °C mean and 108.6 °C standard deviation, versus Test 2, 325.4 °C and 39.5 °C. Those descriptive spatial/channel standard deviations are not model prediction confidence intervals. Pages 9–10 explicitly discuss cross-section, longitudinal, and north/south variation; section-averaged predictions need an identified observation operator for comparison with individual thermocouples.
- The paper repeatedly uses 120-min comparisons. My prior NCSTAR 1-6B review recorded the Test 1 furnace-stop endpoint at 116 min. The present paper does not reconcile that timing/valid-channel issue. This is an unresolved cross-source comparison window, not proof that the late measurements or model are false. No new 1-6B source reading was done in this task.

### Model inputs and measurement dependence — pp. 10–17, 26–27

- The thermal model contains one main truss and a half-width slab, omitting end supports, bridging trusses/angles, knuckle protrusions, and the explicit profiled metal deck. Page 13 approximates the slab with uniform thickness around 130 mm; it does not resolve the original profile. A nominal 19 mm slab-underside SFRM overspray layer is assumed from experimental evidence, not supplied here as an element-by-element measured coating map.
- Equation (1), p. 13, is an energy equation containing density, heat capacity, temperature, an advection term, heat flux divergence, and volumetric source. The implementation is heat transfer using SOLID70 temperature degrees of freedom, LINK33 connections, and SURF152 exchanges, not an accompanying structural equilibrium/compatibility solution. The paper asserts adequate through-depth discretization, but provides no mesh-convergence sequence or native replay package in these pages.
- Pages 14–15 apply the E119 time-temperature history at a reference node; exposed surfaces exchange convection and radiation with that node. This does not impose gas temperature directly as steel temperature. All view factors are set to unity. The selected convection coefficient is 15 W/m²/K, steel emissivity 0.7, and exposed slab emissivity 0.6, with literature and omitted-deck/gap rationale. It would overstate the source to label all these baseline constants demonstrated fits to these test outcomes; it would also overstate it to call them independently measured specimen-specific inputs.
- Page 15 explicitly uses recorded average unexposed-slab thermocouple time histories as time-dependent Dirichlet boundary conditions. It says this boundary condition is the only thermal-model difference between Test 1 and Test 2. Initial temperature is 25 °C; longitudinal ends and unspecified boundaries are adiabatic. Thus the model is a conditional prediction of internal/steel temperatures, not an independent prediction of all measured slab and steel channels or of the thermal consequences of mechanical restraint.
- Page 16 lists substantial assumptions: uniform fireproofing, omitted deck profile/bridging, constant heat-exchange parameters, perfect material contact with indirect gap compensation, no explicit furnace, and no nonuniform furnace-gas field. Pages 16–17 explain section averaging and distance-weighted interpolation to a thermocouple location; an APDL procedure is described but not supplied as executable input.
- Appendix A, pp. 26–27, supplies temperature-dependent steel, concrete, and SFRM property tables attributed to reference 9. This is useful partial input disclosure. Steel property entries end at 702 °C although body tables report higher simulated steel temperatures; the pages do not specify the continuation/extrapolation rule needed to reproduce those results. The SFRM conductivity table prints 0.0 W/m/K at 25 °C at one-decimal precision; that is not evidence that the actual solver input was exactly zero. Native values would distinguish rounding, transcription, and execution choices. Concrete density is printed as 2,101.7 kg/m³ at 23 °C (about 131.2 lb/ft³), not identical to the nominal 100 lb/ft³ concrete description or the Table 2 wet densities. That difference merits provenance/parameter reconciliation, not an inference that one number must be invalid. The SFRM density table also rises above 600 °C; no correction is silently substituted.

### Comparison metrics, tables, and interpretation — pp. 17–21

- Section 4.1 excludes initial transient data because measured heating lags the standard-fire model input. It applies chi-square tests to four lower-chord sensor comparisons, not every thermal output. The paper does not specify a reproducible chi-square residual/variance formula, measurement-error distribution, or a treatment of time-series correlation. Counting time points as independent categories is not by itself a demonstration that the claimed reference distribution applies. Non-rejection is not a probability that the model is true or a calibrated accuracy bound.
- Table 4 (p. 18) is an internal exception to a blanket statement of agreement at both significance levels. Printed rows are: Test 1 A7, df 34, statistic 53.41, 0.005 critical 58.91, 0.01 critical 56.01; Test 1 E3, df 44, 61.44 / 71.86 / 68.67; Test 2 A3, df 41, 67.54 / 68.04 / 64.94; Test 2 E7, df 43, 63.21 / 70.59 / 67.43. Test 2 A3 is below its printed 0.5% threshold but above its printed 1% threshold. The other three rows are below both printed thresholds. This is a direct comparison of the published entries, not a re-computed goodness-of-fit test from raw temperatures.
- Page 18 describes excluding erroneous sensors with reference to the test logs and sometimes comparing a corresponding south-truss sensor when a north-truss sensor behaves anomalously. Those choices may be justified data-quality handling; their exact masks, time windows, and chronology are not provided here. No motive inference follows. There is no declared withheld test/channel/time split or frozen-before-observation parameter protocol in the paper.
- Table 5 (p. 20) is explicitly captioned Test 1. Nearby p. 19 prose invokes Test 2 in a Table 5 comparison, leaving an attribution mismatch; do not invent a second table or silently transfer values between tests. Percent differences are described on an absolute-temperature scale, whereas temperature entries are °C. Consequently a percentage relative to Celsius would misstate the reported metric.
- The claim of within 10% for most lower-chord and mid-web comparisons is more supportable than an all-channels bound. Printed lower-chord E differences include 13.6% at 60 min and 11.6% at 120 min; I is 11.7% at 60 min. Upper-chord A at 90 and 120 min has both observed/model temperatures (382/278 °C and 574/348 °C) but blank percentage cells. Blanks are neither zero errors nor missing pairs.
- A targeted, stdout-only JavaScript arithmetic check, performed after visual transcription and before reading any separate checker result, used `100*(model-observed)/(observed+273.15)`. Mid-web A at 90 min (792/820 °C) gives 2.6287%, versus the printed 3.5%. The two populated-but-blank upper A pairs give −15.8742% and −26.6777% under that same explicitly stated convention. This is arithmetic on printed rounded values, not recovery of the unrounded data or proof of which entry is wrong. It is not a complete Table 5 audit.
- Figures 10–12 and accompanying text show favorable lower/web comparisons alongside clear upper-chord baseline discrepancies. The p. 23 conclusion's broad upper-chord over-prediction wording cannot be applied indiscriminately to baseline Figure 12, where important plotted cases under-predict after abrupt observed heating. It may refer to a different subset/modified scenario; preserve the ambiguity rather than silently changing the source's sign.

### Explicit fitted loss scenarios and what they establish — pp. 21–24

- Pages 21–22 use observed loud-report timing to motivate restart/removal scenarios, with a stated trial-and-error approach to possible SFRM loss. Removal extends 2–3 ft along the model. In Test 1, the printed prescription removes 0.5 in at A and 0.75 in at I/E after 3,800 s; at 4,500 s it removes 0.75 in at C. These are inferred/calibrated model changes, not direct measurements of that spatial loss field.
- Figure 14 (p. 23) compares individual upper sensors: TC5 at A/C and TC1 at I/E. Its C/E intact-SFRM alternatives provide useful within-model counterfactuals. Removal reproduces abrupt heating more plausibly in several cases, but agreement is not uniform and no formal fit metric or independent held-out loss scenario is supplied. Sensor identity matters when comparing Figure 14 with earlier curves.
- The source calls the dislodgement theory verified. The defensible source-level inference is that observed spalling indications plus a fitted removal scenario are consistent with the sudden heating. This is not a uniqueness proof excluding alternative temperature-field, contact, or instrumentation explanations, nor a prediction of loss timing from a coupled mechanics model. Favorable physical observations make the explanation more than an arbitrary curve fit, but they do not measure the fitted removal geometry.
- Pages 23–24 explicitly distinguish predicted and calibrated thermal fields and propose their subsequent use for structural analysis. No sag-time, force, deflection, collapse, or restrained structural FE-versus-test response comparison is executed in these 27 pages. That is a bounded observation about the complete paper, not a claim about every cited report or all NIST work.

## Strongest objections to both overextended readings

**Against dismissing the study:** the steel-channel comparisons are not tautologies simply because unexposed slab measurements are an input; they still test parts of the conduction/exchange model against different measured outputs. The lower/web agreement, stated material-property inputs, disclosed simplifications, and independent physical indications of spalling are meaningful favorable evidence. Failure of one printed significance threshold does not erase all other comparisons. No new execution here falsified the FE solver or NIST's collapse hypothesis.

**Against promoting it to broad validation:** parameter/measurement dependence and explicit loss calibration prevent calling the full exercise blind prediction. Strong upper-channel discrepancies and omitted percentages are not covered by a most-cases lower/web statement. Published chi-square entries and rounded percentage tables are insufficient to authenticate the error model or original calculation. Thermal agreement does not establish force/sag equivalence under different restraint, loading, degradation, or reference conventions, and the later study supplies no historical input/output identity bridge to a particular 2005 structural calculation or WTC 7 member conditions.

## Smallest concrete follow-up that could change this assessment

The paper itself identifies Banerjee and Gross, *A Study of Thermal Behavior of Composite Floor System in Standard Fire Resistance Tests*, NIST Technical Note 1771 (2012), DOI `10.6028/NIST.TN.1771`, as reference 9 (p. 25), including the source for Appendix A properties. It is a specific uninspected lead, not newly retrieved evidence. The most discriminating next records are the native thermal input/property continuation rules, measured unexposed boundary histories, exact steel-channel/time masks including late Test 1 validity, and separate baseline/removal outputs with tuning chronology. Those would permit a fixed observation-operator residual check and distinguish assumed baseline inputs from fitted choices. A sag-transfer claim additionally needs the identified structural calculation and matched specimen/load/restraint/sensor/time mapping; this paper's thermal result alone does not supply it. No further acquisition or execution is authorized by this recommendation.
