### Truss geometry, boundaries and model-development scope

Printed 69–72 (physical 117–120), §5.4.1–5.4.3, describe a single long-span C32T1 floor-truss section at WTC 1 Floor 96, Column 143, with a 40-in. slab strip, neighboring column/spandrel/strap-anchor pieces, truss seats and symmetry. Figure 5-25 labels the model span 713 in. and column length 288 in. This is not one of the 35-ft/17-ft physical furnace specimens reviewed in NCSTAR 1-6B, nor a WTC 7 assembly.

The report documents simplifying assumptions rather than hiding them: a 4.35-in. equivalent isotropic concrete slab gives about 15% higher transverse bending stiffness, accepted because transverse bending was considered small; deck and welded-wire fabric are not represented explicitly or implicitly in this truss model's concrete law; a 2-in. camber and the viscoelastic damper are omitted. Beam elements have temperature-dependent elastic, plastic and creep properties. Contact retains compression after knuckle break-element failure. These facts locate modeling assumptions; their effect on historical response is not quantified here by this reader.

The top chord has continuous lateral restraint; the bottom chord is laterally restrained at four bridging locations. The interior seat is fixed, the exterior seat attaches to the spandrel, and the interior slab is initially restrained longitudinally through break elements with a tensile capacity. Column ends have specified rotation/translation restraints. Printed 72 explicitly states that column boundary conditions affect horizontal thermal reaction and refers to §5.5.16, unreviewed in this pass. Printed 71 says symmetry prevents this truss model's application to lateral-load cases; the full-floor model is said to capture bridging/primary-truss interaction. That is a reported later-model distinction, not verified here.

### Temperature history and model checks are not furnace input data

Printed 73 (physical 121), §5.4.4, explicitly says the temperature history was used to exercise the model and was not obtained from fire dynamics or heat-conduction analyses. It ramps from 20°C to 700°C in steel and slab bottom, and to 300°C at slab top, in 30 minutes; thereafter maxima rise 20°C/min. A linear slab-through-thickness gradient is assumed. No temperature load is applied to columns or spandrel. This is neither measured furnace temperature input nor an end-to-end predicted fire/heat-transfer history.

Gravity loads are structural weight plus 8 psf superimposed dead load and 13.75 psf live load (25% of a 55 psf design live load). Construction sequence is represented by applying wet-slab/truss self-weight before adding a stress-free concrete slab and the other loads. These are not the full-design furnace loading histories from NCSTAR 1-6B.

Printed 73–74 (physical 121–122) also state that concrete post-cracking/crushing negative slope was removed to improve convergence, and that this truss creep formulation used a room-temperature yield-strength ratio, 35.5/σ_yRT, later changed by NIST to the ultimate-strength ratio 70.5/σ_uRT. Do not silently treat all model versions as identical. Table 5-9 attributes resistance-weld strengths to Laclede test data; this is a physical-data attribution within the selected section, not an independently inspected raw test record.

Printed 75 (physical 123), §5.4.7, compares maximum dead/live-load vertical displacement with a single truss extracted from the ANSYS full-floor model: reported maximum difference 3.5%, with construction-sequence effects excluded from both. This checks consistency between models, not furnace prediction accuracy. The nearby debris-load exercise uses an earlier developmental model with different slab properties, boundaries and omitted break elements. Printed 75–76 report termination at load factor 3.4 and knuckle failures at factors 2.4 and 2.8. The plotted reactions described as measured in prose are model results, not physical instrumentation data.

### Thermal response, assumptions and favorable/adverse evidence

Printed 77–83 (physical 125–131) report calculated truss response to the assumed thermal history. The static solution switches to dynamic analysis with 5% Rayleigh damping to overcome nonconvergence, then resumes static analysis when velocity/acceleration become small. No solver replay or stability/sensitivity assessment was performed here. The figure's zero vertical-displacement reference is the state after self-weight; it is not a universal cold unloaded zero.

The report describes end-diagonal yielding around 445°C, web-diagonal buckling around 565°C, interior seat-bolt shear at 566°C without losing vertical support, and later exterior connection failures/walk-off. Printed 81 explicitly warns that actual tower failure modes and sequence could differ because actual and assumed histories differ. It says later simplified/full-floor analysis used estimates of the actual histories; that later calculation is not inspected here. These modeled temperature thresholds must not be converted into measured historical temperatures or WTC 7 predictions.

There are numerical qualifications in the printed narrative: printed 77 says the analysis proceeded to 727°C, while printed 81 describes walk-off at 730°C; its prose uses 670°C for gusset fracture, followed by bolt shear, while its summary groups those at around 680°C. These can be rounding or event-grouping descriptions; the selected pages do not resolve exact execution values. They do not justify a causal or misconduct inference.

The detailed model's strap-anchor weld failure was not represented by break elements: at about 700°C the report compares approximately 10 kip tension to 7.5 kip weld strength and says including that failure could cause earlier walk-off. That is a stated model limitation and directional possibility, not an observed earlier failure.

Printed 84 (physical 132), §5.4.10, is a second model-to-model comparison: simplified versus detailed truss under gravity and the same assumed thermal history. It aims to capture both midspan vertical displacement and total horizontal reaction. Favorable evidence is the reported qualitative/overall agreement and the explicit use of two distinct response metrics, not only sag. Important qualifications are also explicit: simplified-model web buckling at 530°C versus about 565°C; approximately 2 kip versus 10 kip tension at 700°C. Agreement in overall response does not imply close agreement in every local event or force.

The simplified representation preserves truss geometry, doubles sections to combine a truss pair, uses one BEAM188 element per member, changes failure-element representation, omits knuckle break elements with a stated small-effect rationale, omits construction sequence, and gives the slab the same yield strength in tension and compression (set to compression strength). Creep is included in the simplified truss comparison but explicitly omitted when incorporated in the full-floor model because of convergence problems. Consequently even simplified-truss agreement cannot silently certify the subsequently incorporated full-floor model under all loads/temperatures. Figure 5-38 is on the next page, not yet viewed at this freeze; this paragraph checks the printed 84 prose only.

### Component physical-test comparison is present

Printed 61–62 (physical 109–110), §5.3.3, describe 1967 Laclede experiments in longitudinal and transverse shear of knuckles. These are actual physical-test reports attributed by NIST, not merely comparisons between two computer models. This is material favorable evidence against any sweeping assertion that the report has no physical component-test basis.

The test-to-building transfer is explicit rather than one-to-one. For longitudinal shear the report distinguishes the specification from photographs (confining angles and reinforcement), normal-weight test concrete from lightweight office-floor concrete, unexplained lower strength in a later cylinder, and weld failure in the third test. The two retained tests give approximately 28.3 kip/knuckle; the report scales by 4,100/3,707 psi to about 31 kip for mature in-place concrete. For transverse shear it gives approximately 16.9 kip/knuckle and scales by 4,100/1,965 psi to about 35 kip. These are report-described adjusted capacities, not direct measurements of the as-built tower under fire.

The printed 61 wording alternates seven-day and six-day descriptions in the transverse-test paragraph. Cylinder versus assembly ages may be part of the distinction, but I did not resolve the wording against original laboratory records. Figures 5-17 and 5-18 reproduce Laclede test sheets within the report; the full pages were viewed, but fine embedded handwriting/numerals were not all readable at the delivered view size and were not transcribed or certified. No underlying original sheet or drawing was separately accessed. The reported test limitations above come from the readable NIST body text, not an independent reconstruction of those sheets.

Printed 63–66 (physical 111–114), §5.3.4–5.3.6, describe quarter-specimen solid-element models, imposed displacement ramps, a concrete Pseudo Tensor law, 0.47% distributed reinforcement, concrete strengths 4,100 psi longitudinally and 2,500 psi transversely, and two steel/concrete interface assumptions: fully bonded and frictionless. The prose identifies LS-DYNA as the analysis program; stress images display ANSYS labels. I have not resolved whether that reflects preprocessing/postprocessing, an alternate calculation or a reporting inconsistency, and make no solver-identity or execution finding from the labels alone.

The report gives longitudinal calculated strengths of 15–35 kip per knuckle depending on interface condition, with the observed comparison judged closer to fully bonded. Transverse frictionless analysis gives about 24 kip for 2,500 psi concrete, scaled to 39 kip for 4,100 psi; the fully bonded analysis stops at 20 kip per knuckle before ultimate strength. The report concludes that the sensitivity analyses support the shear capacities inferred from tests. It also says the transverse model's UY=0 boundary is realistic for the test but would not occur for the pair of actual trusses. This is a physically informed component comparison with acknowledged assumptions, not a blind held-out floor prediction or an ultimate-capacity result for the prematurely terminated bonded case.

### The heated-capacity step is an assumed/calculated transfer

Printed 66–67 (physical 114–115) expressly base temperature effects on hand calculations and engineering judgment. The gas/steel/concrete temperature relationships are assumed, differential thermal expansion is neglected in the described estimate, and the room-temperature pullout capacity is estimated at 15 kip. Table 5-7 maps gas temperatures 20–450/650/850/1,050°C to steel 20–375/550/725/900°C and concrete 20–300/450/600/750°C; the corresponding shear capacities are 30/24/19/15 kip and pullout capacities 15/12/10/7 kip. This is not a reported heated-knuckle test series or measured temperature-to-capacity dataset. The room-temperature shear-test comparison supplies some physical basis but does not independently validate every elevated-temperature assumption.

Printed 67 additionally specifies no vertical-compression capacity limit and independence of that compression resistance from capacities in other directions. The described knuckle model uses 15 break and five beam elements; Figure 5-24 lies outside this extension and was not viewed. These assumptions must not be generalized into a complete floor-furnace validation or WTC 7 connection validation.
