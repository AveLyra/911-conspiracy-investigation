### Method limits and favorable evidence

Printed65 distinguishes nominal manufacturer properties from the new
thermophysical tests. Printed66–68 identifies sample preparation and the
hot-wire method. At1200°C MK-5 shrinkage exposed the wire and prevented its
conductivity measurement; the corresponding table cell is a dash, not a zero.
Printed72 records pushrod-contact loss around1100°C, and printed73/Table6-7
only lists expansion through1000°C. The1200°C density's presence does not
supply a measured1200°C expansion entry or overcome the explicit600°C limit.
No missing value is reconstructed.

The source openly discusses these difficulties. Printed71 notes milligram
sample representativeness, possible mass loss and procedural causes of
different DSC peaks; both compared studies used5°C/min. Printed74–75 states
that application affects density/conductivity and that variation among
samples is expected; it does not quantify a measured between-sample distribution.
It points to promising methods and future work, not an already
validated replacement law. Reading the continuation supplied no exception to
the600°C density warning. References119–122 identify cited methods/literature;
their listing is not a fresh standards-compliance or cited-paper audit.

The strongest objection to treating the density issue as proof of a bad
calculation is that a report's table does not show precisely what the solver
used, whether it clamped or changed a law, whether relevant protection reached
the disputed range, or how strongly the output depended on it. Conversely,
the strongest objection to dismissing the issue as harmless rounding is that
the source's temperature-domain warning is explicit and independent of the
small inter-report numeric offsets. Both objections must remain visible.

Real laboratory work, transparent limitations and useful reported steel-
temperature agreement remain affirmative support for parts of the thermal
method. None establishes every material law, the loaded structural response,
