# Plasco full-text comparator follow-up

Research working note, 2026-09-08 to 2026-09-09. This is a bounded WP4/Q08 follow-up under the investigation charter (supporting item not included in this public package), not a case fact, expert report, causal verdict, or completed comparator study. It supplements the earlier primary-source pilot (supporting item not included in this public package). No SRC, FACT, or EXH identifier is created.

Two public institutional manuscripts now permit examination beyond abstracts. The first, despite its title, tests fire spread and member temperatures; the second constructs an image-calibrated fire history and member heat-transfer examples. Neither presents a completed simulation of Plasco's global progressive collapse. The accessible work demonstrates sensitivity and offers candidate thermal histories, but does not uniquely identify the historical failure sequence. A 2025 structural follow-up was located but its full PDF was not acquired in this batch; its abstract is kept separate below.

## Provenance and investigation dependencies

P5 p.3 explicitly obtains architecture, ventilation, and fuel information from Ahmadi et al. (2020) and Behnam (2019). Its building/member description depends on their reconstructions after missing drawings (pp.5–6). Fire-load density 1910 MJ/m² is taken from Behnam; nylon is selected as a tractable surrogate, not identified by a recovered-material inventory (p.7). The reported origin and early spread also depend on TFSD's 2017 Persian report and Ferdowsizadeh's *Panjshanbeh Suri (Thursday Fireworks)*, listed at p.22. Photographs in P5 come through news/photo sources listed at pp.22–23, not a disclosed set of original camera files.

P6 pp.6–8 constructs its FireEvolutionMap and timing intervals from TFSD, Ahmadi, the documentary, visual records, and firefighters' interviews. It then uses those intervals to assign fuel and remove false ceilings/windows in the model. This is a useful explicit dependency chain, but the paper does not give this audit direct access to the underlying TFSD file, original interview records, full photograph custody, or initial drawings. TFSD's report should not silently be equated with every national committee report: those are separately cited documents in the earlier pilot. The national fire-engineering and structural-report URLs were re-attempted through the web tool and returned non-retryable unsafe errors; they were not retried by another route.

P5 and P6 share several authors and documentary inputs. P6 extends the same research programme and discusses earlier structural models; it does not supply an independent second historical dataset. The P6 publisher's reference list also identifies P5. Neither repeated institutional hosting nor repeated use of the same incident imagery provides an unused validation set.

P6 p.16 reuses the calibrated floor-10 fire for floors 11 and 14. Floor 14's limited visible fire receives alternative explanations, including suppression or failure to enter adjoining compartments. Floors 12 and 13 are said to have broadly similar visible fire, but floor 12 is calibrated separately for thermal inputs; floor 15 is also separately calibrated. The floor-10 run burns all fuel placed in two NW compartments for 90 minutes and **does not model suppression**. The placement limits substitute for portions of the actual suppression/spread process. Lower-floor fires after the second collapse are not calibrated. These choices can be reasonable approximations for exploration, but their structural consequences remain to be quantified before using the outputs as uniquely historical temperature fields.

## Negative cases, alternative paths, and validation

The important negative result in P5 is **non-spread**, not non-collapse. At the same specified fire load and ventilation, carton-like Case 2 yields hotter and longer local exposure but does not ignite upper floors in the two-floor test (p.17), and does not reach the adjacent compartment in the horizontal test (p.19). Case 1 spreads in both directions but produces lower compartment/member temperatures. Because Case 2 failed to reproduce vertical spread, the authors did not run its six-floor NW reconstruction (p.17). Their mixed-distribution explanation on pp.19–20 is therefore a hypothesis prompted by the sensitivity, not an independently measured inventory or a separately validated mixed-case global model.

P5's bench experiment compares wood blocks with sticks under 50 kW/m² irradiation, using oxygen calorimetry (pp.13–14). It supports a qualitative packing/burning-rate effect. The authors expressly limit it to a qualitative indication and request compartment-scale experiments. It does not validate Plasco's nylon composition, actual packing, ventilation, or temperatures. No repeat count or error bars are supplied in the displayed experiment. General prior experiments and agreement with empirical relations are relevant physical checks, but they are different from historical-event validation.

P6 preserves additional alternatives: in the NW vertical model, high-temperature gases rise in the stair shaft but do **not** ignite the upper-floor fuel; windows/openings supply the modeled ignition path (pp.13–14). For floor 13's NE nonappearance of fire, the authors list external suppression, intact boundaries, and insufficient ceiling fuel, then choose a reduced-fuel representation for calibration (p.18). Those alternatives can produce similar visible façades with different internal heating. A discriminating audit should vary them rather than equate the chosen no-fuel region with direct evidence of no historic fuel or no interior heat.

P6 Fig.4 (p.6) groups images into broad time ranges; Fig.5 (p.7) gives an inferred chronology ending about 219 minutes after ignition. Its repeated prose shorthand of collapse in three hours should not replace that figure with an exact 180-minute measurement. No new calibrated video timing is supplied here. Both studies use the same incident imagery to define and assess spread. P6 explicitly describes repeated tuning of obstruction times and fuel placement. Neither inspected manuscript declares unseen cameras, reserved time intervals, a predeclared error metric, independent temperature measurements, or an event-level holdout. Their image match is retrospective compatibility/calibration.

## Internal inconsistencies requiring version checks

P5 p.13 assigns its solid wood block to Case 1 and sticks to Case 2, opposite the earlier loose-stack/carton mapping. Fig.12 identifies the physical specimens as block/sticks; this report uses those identities and does not propagate the reversed labels. On p.15 the prose assigns roughly 560°C / 410°C at slab depth 23 mm to Cases 1 / 2 respectively, while Fig.13(b) shows Case 2 as the upper curve and Case 1 as the lower. The figure's 0–60 horizontal axis is labelled seconds even though pp.14–15 describe an hour-long analysis. Neither the swapped text values nor a literal 60-second reading is adopted as verified thermal data. P5's 120/130 mm slab difference is preserved above.

P6 p.19's corner descriptions switch among northeast/southwest/southeast while Fig.14 and p.20 identify northwest/southeast traces. The report takes the figure labels as the reported trace labels and flags the prose mismatch rather than inventing a geometry correction. These are manuscript-level observations, not claims that the final editions contain the same errors. Raw histories and final editions are needed before quantitative reuse.

## Relationship to the earlier 2020/2021 abstracts

The previous pilot's P1 (*Structures* 28, 205–224, DOI 10.1016/j.istruc.2020.08.058) selects a collapse scenario against photographs/video; P2 (Aghakouchak et al., e1815) reports numerical structural mechanisms and image agreement. Those remain abstract/snippet-level inspections here; the present two full fire papers do **not** upgrade them to checked full structural methods. P6 pp.3–4 itself criticizes fixed/idealized temperatures in earlier structural work and motivates reconstructed fire histories. That criticism is the authors' methodological position, not this audit's reproduction of an earlier model failure.

The previous pilot's P3 (Ahmadi et al.) and P4 (Behnam) are upstream sources for P5/P6, not independent new observations created by citation. P4's previously inspected abstract reports failure under an estimated 1900 MJ/m² case and no structural failure at 511 MJ/m², with columns retaining capacity under modeled masonry protection. Preserve that result as **abstract-level** sensitivity. P5 uses 1910 MJ/m² and does not rerun that 511 MJ/m² comparison; P6's no-coating statement does not resolve P4's masonry assumption.

P7's 2025 abstract describes a sequential FDS/heat-transfer/thermomechanical study of **partial NW-corner collapse**, proposing prolonged exposure, restraint, differential column movements and beam-column connection rotation/failure. It also reports that elevated NW peripheral-column temperatures did not compromise global stability. That is a valuable next full-text discriminator and should prevent treating every fire model as a universal total-collapse result. At this access level, it cannot adjudicate actual connection failure, supply a global-collapse validation, or support an independently reproduced survival calculation.

## Narrow comparator disposition and next check

Keep Plasco as a **fire-associated collapse case with investigator attribution and assumption-dependent reconstructions**. The expanded record permits specific questions about compartmentation, fuel height/packing, opening histories, suppression, and thermal gradients. It does not validate NIST's WTC 7 initiation/propagation sequence, exclude every intervention alternative, or make Plasco a matched causal control. P6 p.11 itself cautions that building size, spread and protection differ and that each building requires its own investigation.

The next bounded check is the full P7 published PDF, including its actual NW scope, failure criteria, whole-building boundary conditions, stability statement, negative runs, and dependence on P6's thermal calibration. Follow that with the P6 detailed FireEvolutionMap/supplementary files and exact FDS/OpenSees decks, versions, property tables, control-device schedules, all tested cases and raw thermal histories. A useful falsification test holds the exterior observations fixed while varying plausible interior fuel, suppression and compartment-failure histories, then asks whether the structural mechanism is robust. Mere visual fit is insufficient to answer that question.
