# Flight 93: an Airfone center's reported local 911 call

**Finding.** A 2006 coauthored memoir reports that the Airfone surveillance center contacted a **local 911 operator** about the incident. The narrator explicitly says she learned that detail later. Separately, an FAA transcript reports that a passenger's purported 911 air-phone call reached Oak Brook police. These are concrete records to compare; **the available evidence does not establish that the two accounts refer to the same 911 call**.

The center name is **“Airplane Operations Surveillance Center”** in the 2004 memorandum and **“Airfone Operations Surveillance Center”** in the 2006 book. Preserve both labels with their sources. They have the same acronym, but identity, correct expansion and the reason for the difference have not been established. The 2004 memorandum's “perhaps other authorities” does not exclude a 911 contact; nor does the later book verify one by itself. The accounts are not independent measurements merely because they appear in different documents.

## Open hypotheses, not findings

- **Untested:** the corporate center's reported local 911 call and the FAA relay refer to the same information chain, with one or more intervening relays.
- **Untested:** they refer to separate calls or reports, or the apparent overlap arose from another source or reporting error.

Neither account establishes whether a passenger directly dialed 911. Do not convert the possible corporate-to-911 leg into a verified reconciliation or choose the actual device from the reported terminology. A direct police recording, dispatch CAD/event log and original intake notes; the company's surveillance-center watch and phone logs; the original operator notes; and the FAA Great Lakes notification and call-transfer records could test the chain. Record agency, clock, line, caller and event IDs separately before attempting any join. A distinct New York State Police ground 911 call in the existing investigation remains a separate record unless actual evidence connects it.
