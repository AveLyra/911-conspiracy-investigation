# Plain-English assessment: CIA and FAA/NORAD/Mineta leads

October 26, 2026. Interpretive companion to the [source-checked review](https://github.com/AveLyra/911-conspiracy-investigation/blob/0b0ab462ea57660478f2388c2feab173e47d10a8/investigation/broader-evidence/outstanding-records-verification-2026-10-26/REPORT.md). This records the explanation, relative lead strengths, competing explanations and retrieval priorities requested in chat. Priority rankings are research judgments, not new historical facts or source adjudications. The published master remains at 175 overlapping entries; this supplement adds no new master IDs.

**The strongest lead concerns officials leaving an inaccurate account uncorrected after learning it was wrong.** That warrants investigating possible concealment of failures after the attacks. The new records do not yet establish coordinated concealment, advance knowledge or participation in the attacks.

1. **FAA officials knew a particular answer was wrong—and did not correct it.**

   The FAA manages civilian air traffic; NORAD handles military air defence. An FAA answer to the 9/11 Commission claimed that its Air Force liaison joined an important telephone conference immediately after the first tower was struck, around **8:46 a.m.** The Transportation Inspector General’s review says she joined **after the Pentagon strike at 9:37**. It found that three executives subsequently learned the answer was inaccurate, but none corrected it with the Commission. [DOT report, pp. 9–10](https://www.oig.dot.gov/sites/default/files/cc2006085.pdf#page=9)

   **Why it matters:** this goes beyond discovering an innocent error. It identifies reported knowledge of the error followed by noncorrection. It is our strongest new accountability lead.

   **What could weaken a conspiracy interpretation:** the executives described assumptions that someone else would correct it; that differs from an agreement to suppress the truth. The report recommended considering administrative action, but we have not obtained the final disposition. [DOT report, p. 12](https://www.oig.dot.gov/sites/default/files/cc2006085.pdf#page=12)

   **Next records:** the answer’s drafts and approvals, communications after the liaison challenged it, original interview records, any correction sent, and the final administrative-action decision.

2. **An unverified CIA assurance that “the FBI was told” may have influenced later work.**

   This concerns travel information about Khalid al-Mihdhar, subsequently identified as one of the hijackers. [DOJ review, Chapter Five](https://oig.justice.gov/sites/default/files/archive/special/0506/chapter5.htm)

   A CIA internal message dated **January 5, 2000**, identified as **SCALEC 134684**, asserted that his travel documents had reached the FBI. The CIA review reports that an FBI employee working at the CIA accessed that message on **July 2, 2001**. It suggests she may consequently have approached later work believing the FBI already knew relevant information. Importantly, that explanation of her thinking is the review’s **inference**, not an acquired statement from her. [CIA review, printed p. 67; mirrored copy](https://documents.theblackvault.com/documents/cia/DOC_0006184107.pdf#page=92)

   **Why it matters:** an unsupported assurance can discourage follow-up. The question is whether this was mistaken information spreading through an organisation, undocumented genuine delivery, or knowingly misleading documentation.

   **What could weaken suspicion:** a contemporaneous FBI receipt, an identifiable informal handoff, or records showing that the employee’s later decisions did not depend on that assurance. A separate passage discusses possible paper-copy use, demonstrating why electronic access logs alone cannot settle every delivery question. It does **not** prove delivery of this January message. [CIA review, printed p. 68](https://documents.theblackvault.com/documents/cia/DOC_0006184107.pdf#page=93)

   **Next records:** the actual cable, its distribution list, FBI incoming records, July access/tasking emails, print records and the August 23 intelligence report.

3. **Official accounts materially changed which aircraft prompted military action—and when.**

   Earlier accounts associated **9:24 a.m.** with notification about American 77, the Pentagon aircraft. The Commission’s later account says that notification concerned an erroneous report that **American 11 was still airborne**. Similarly, May 2003 testimony placed notification about United 93 at **9:16**, whereas the later Commission account gives **10:07**, after its crash. These are substantial differences in the accounts, not merely a few seconds of rounding. [Commission Staff Statement 17, pp. 17–19](https://govinfo.library.unt.edu/911/staff_statements/staff_statement_17.pdf#page=17)

   **Why it matters:** the versions give different pictures of what military personnel knew and what their actions answered.

   **What could weaken deliberate-fabrication suspicions:** contemporaneous records showing confusion, mistaken aircraft identification and subsequent good-faith correction. DOT found insufficient evidence of knowing falsity concerning hijack notifications, while also finding the explanation for omitting United 93’s time unreasonable. Those findings must remain together. [DOT report, pp. 5–8](https://www.oig.dot.gov/sites/default/files/cc2006085.pdf#page=5)

   **Next records:** original notification calls, operational logs and chronology drafts, with evidence showing how their clocks were checked.

4. **Mineta’s recollection conflicts with the Commission’s later White House timeline.**

   Transportation Secretary Norman Mineta testified that he reached the White House emergency bunker around **9:20**, then heard an approaching-aircraft discussion five or six minutes later. He identified the aircraft as the one that struck the Pentagon. But he also explicitly acknowledged that interpreting the unspecified order as a shootdown order was his inference. [May 23, 2003 testimony](https://govinfo.library.unt.edu/911/archive/hearing2/9-11Commission_Hearing_2003-05-23.htm)

   The Commission placed Cheney’s arrival in the bunker conference room around **9:58**, and described later approaching-aircraft exchanges. It acknowledged conflicting evidence. We have not established that these descriptions concern the same exchange. [Staff Statement 17, pp. 23–25](https://govinfo.library.unt.edu/911/staff_statements/staff_statement_17.pdf#page=23)

   **Why it matters:** this is a specific witness-versus-reconstruction conflict worth testing. The testimony alone does not identify a stand-down instruction.

   **What could weaken suspicion:** mistaken recollection, different locations or separate aircraft discussions. Conversely, independently timed records supporting Mineta would challenge the later reconstruction.

   **Next records:** original bunker logs, White House telephone logs, witness notes and hearing audio/video. The Commission’s note says the alarm data supporting the **9:37 tunnel-entry time** were no longer retrievable; we need the retention and retrieval history before suggesting deliberate destruction. [Final-report note 209, printed p. 464](https://www.9-11commission.gov/report/911Report_Notes.pdf#page=16)

**My retrieval priorities would be:** the FAA knowledge-and-correction trail first; the CIA cable-and-recipient trail second; and the original White House and military timing records third. The identified archival folders provide concrete search routes, although their contents remain unexamined.

Several other findings mainly constrain our investigation: Mineta’s testimony remains publicly available; six audio CDs versus 21 downloadable channels is not inherently contradictory; and our automatic transcription failed its silence control. Those findings help prevent us from mistaking an access problem, format difference or our own software error for historical evidence.
